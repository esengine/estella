Estella runs full-screen post-processing through ping-pong framebuffers. Add a
`PostProcessVolume` component, enable the effects you want, and tune their
parameters — in the editor or in code. Effects share the ping-pong buffers, so
several can stack in one frame at no extra target cost beyond the passes.

### Anti-aliasing before the chain

Geometry edges are smoothed by **MSAA on the scene itself**, not by an effect: 4×
by default, on WebGL2 and on WebGPU — which is what every native build (iOS,
Android, desktop, Steam) runs on. `createWebApp({ msaaSamples })` sizes it, and
`msaaSamples: 1` turns it off where the fill cost matters more than the edges.
`fxaa` is the cheaper, blurrier alternative for devices where even one sample
extra is too much.

### Motion blur and the lens

`motionBlur` needs no setup: add it and it follows the camera by the depth buffer and any moving mesh by the same
motion pass temporal anti-aliasing uses, so a mesh that moves by itself is smeared along its own path and not the
camera's. `intensity` is the shutter — 0.5 is the half-frame exposure of a 180° shutter, 1 a whole frame — and
`maxBlur` caps the streak as a fraction of the picture so a cut or a teleport smears a little and not the frame.
It draws the picture still when the camera has not moved, and it does not shift the picture sub-pixel the way a
temporal effect does, so it can be used without `taa`.

`filmGrain` and `lensFlare` are plain post effects. The flare reads the picture itself, so it only has something to
throw where the picture holds values above `threshold`: a bright sun disc, a lamp, an emissive surface (in HDR).

### The sky and thin geometry

`skyAtmosphere` draws the sky after the scene, under it. For that to leave a wire or a railing thinner than a pixel whole, the
scene is cleared to nothing while the effect is in the chain and its alpha says how much of each pixel geometry covers, so the
sky is laid under an edge by that share instead of replacing the pixel. Nothing downstream sees it: the effect hands back alpha 1.
Turn **Keep Background** up and the scene is cleared to the camera's colour again, which is what keeping the background means.

### Cloud shadows

Turn **Cloud Shadows** above 0 and the sun's ray from each surface is marched up through the cloud layer the sky draws, against the
same density, so the ground is darker and patchier under a cloud and lit where the sky is open. The sun's own light is what is
kept off; ambient light is not shadowed. Casting shadows also pins the clouds to the world instead of carrying them along with the
camera, so walking under a cloud and out of it works. The sky is in kilometres and the scene in its own units: **World Units per km**
says how many of the scene's units make one. At 0 (the default) the effect is exactly as it was. The shadow falls on surfaces only; the
clouds are still drawn where there is sky.

### Rain, wet ground and puddles

`rain` is a storm in two parts. **Falling rain** is three layers of thin slanted streaks scrolling down the
screen, each standing at its own distance: a streak is hidden by a surface nearer than its layer and drawn
over one farther away, and the sky counts as infinitely far. **Wet ground** is found from the depth buffer —
a surface that faces up, flat enough to hold water — and darkened by `wetness`, because water in the pores takes
the light a dry surface scatters. Drops that land on it leave **ripples** (rings that spread and fade) and
**splashes** (the small quick crown of a drop on ground that is not water), laid out on a grid in the world so they
stay on the ground when the camera moves. Ripples need drops: with `intensity` at 0 the ground is wet and still.

**Puddles** are standing water: `puddles` is how much of the flat ground is under it and `puddleScale` how big the
pools are, a world length. A pool is darker still, takes denser and clearer ripples, and no splashes. Where a puddle
lies is a function of the world position and of the ground around it: the noise places the pools, and the depth buffer
says which places are low — a point lower than the ground within a pool's reach holds more water, one on a rise holds
less, and flat ground keeps the coverage it was given. Pools therefore gather against a kerb, in a dip and at the foot
of a slope, and they do not move with the camera. A puddle is also a *mirror*, and
that is the reflections effect's part: give `ssr` the same `puddles` and `puddleScale` and the pools the rain
darkens are the pools that reflect — flat, whatever the ground under them, and far more reflective than a dry surface,
with a sharp reflection where a dry one is blurred. Wet ground in general is shinier than dry: raise `ssr`'s `intensity`
when it rains.

**Shelter.** Rain does not fall through a roof. While a `rain` effect is in the chain the frame draws the scene once from straight
above, depth only, in a 1024² square around the camera (`shelterRange` world units each way) — a map of the highest surface at
each place — and the effect asks it whether something stands over a point. Ground under an awning stays dry and still (no
wetness, no puddles, no ripples), the streaks of a layer that stands under a roof are not drawn, and the drops that would have
fallen there have landed on the awning, which is open to the sky and is wet in its turn. It costs the depth-only draw of the
resident meshes in range, so keep `shelterRange` to the reach you need; ground outside the map is treated as open.

The clock is the game's. A capture that has to be the same on every run sets `timeScale` to 0 and `phase` to the time it
wants. What this is not: the ground's shape is read from what the camera sees (a hollow behind a wall is not known),
and the rain is a screen-space layer, not particles that collide with the world.

### Subsurface scattering

Skin, wax, marble and leaves show the lit picture softened along the surface, each colour by a different amount: red
travels far, blue barely. `subsurface` is that blur, as an x pass and a y pass over the finished picture. Which
surfaces take part is the material's choice — a Model material with `u_subsurface` above zero marks its pixels (it
draws them with alpha 0.996, which nothing on screen shows and which the effect gives back as 1) — and the effect
decides how far: `width` is a world length for the widest colour, `color` the proportions between the channels (the
brightest travels the full width), `strength` how much of the blur is mixed in. A marked pixel gathers only from marked
neighbours on the same surface, so an ear does not bleed into the wall behind it.

It blurs the lit colour as it stands, specular included, so a sharp highlight on a scattering surface is softened with
it, and the width belongs to the effect, so every scattering material in view shares it. The effect needs scene depth
like ambient occlusion does, and the low quality level leaves it out.

### Ambient occlusion

Two estimators share one blur and one composite. `ssao` counts how many of a ring of nearby points stand above the
surface; it is the cheaper of the two and needs a `bias` so that a flat floor does not shade itself. `gtao` marches
the depth buffer along a few slice planes through each pixel, finds how high the horizon climbs on both sides, and
integrates the arc that is left open, weighted by the surface's own normal — so there is no bias to set, a flat floor
stays white, and a crease darkens by how closed it really is. `radius` is a world length in both. `thickness` (GTAO) lets
a thin occluder such as a railing be seen past. Both read scene depth, run at half resolution and blur without crossing
a depth edge. Quality levels govern both through the one ambient-occlusion switch, and the low level leaves GTAO out.

### Contact shadows and bounce light

`contactShadows` marches a short ray from each pixel toward the sun (the scene's own directional light, like the fog) in the depth
buffer, `length` world units long, and darkens the pixel by `intensity` where the ray passes just behind what the depth buffer shows
(`thickness` says how thick a thing may be and still count). It is for what a shadow map is too coarse to hold — a lamp's foot, a fold of
cloth, the crack under a door — and is laid over the finished picture, so it is a correction at the contact and not a second shadow.

`ssgi` is one bounce of **diffuse light** from what the camera can see. It needs the [G-buffer](#the-g-buffer): from each surface eight
rays leave over its hemisphere around the stored normal, each is marched six steps up to `radius` in the depth buffer, and what it lands on
gives out its colour (the scene's, so the bounce feeds itself over frames). The gather runs at half size into a float target; the second pass averages
the neighbours that lie on the same surface and adds the light times the receiver's own colour, so a red wall tints the white floor
beside it and a grey wall bounces grey. It is a screen-space estimate — it sees only what is on screen, and a light that moves lights its
surroundings with it, which a baked light cannot do — and it is noisier than it looks in one frame: `taa` averages what is left.

### Auto exposure

`autoExposure` is the eye: it measures how bright the picture is and settles the exposure that puts
it at middle grey. The measurement is the average of the **log** of the luminance, since brightness
is judged by ratio and a raw average is decided by whatever lamp is in view; pixels darker than 2^-8
are left out, because a black backdrop is not a dark scene. The result moves toward its target over time
from where the last frame left it — brighter faster (`speedUp`) than darker (`speedDown`), per second, so it does not
depend on the frame rate — and `minEV` / `maxEV` bound how far it may go. `bias` adds stops to the result for a
scene that should sit darker or brighter than grey. `metering` chooses where the picture is measured: 0 all of it, 1
weighted toward the middle (a soft falloff of radius `meterSize`), 2 a spot — a disc of that radius at the middle and
nothing else, so a bright window behind a dark face stops deciding the face's exposure. The measurement and the settled
exposure are kept in float targets of their own wherever the device can render to one, so it works the same in an
8-bit chain, where the picture it exposes is still clamped. Put it before the output transform and before `bloom`,
so the glow sees the exposure the eye settled on.

### Depth of field

`depthOfField` blurs what is not at the focus, leaving a slab `focusRange` deep sharp. It is three passes: the first settles
where the lens is focused, the second measures how blurred each pixel is (stored beside the colour, in a float target),
the third gathers the blur.

**Focus** is `focusDistance`, a world length from the camera — or, with `autoFocus` on, the distance of the surface under
the point `focusX`, `focusY` (shares of the screen's width from the left and height from the top, nine taps around it so one
pixel does not decide). It moves toward that distance at `focusSpeed`, in the log of the distance, so a rack focus is a
glide; a point with nothing but sky under it leaves the lens where it was.

**The blur** radius, in pixels, grows with how far a surface is from the focus as a share of its distance, up to `maxBlur`;
`aperture` is the radius of something infinitely far, and the sky counts as that. Each pixel gathers its neighbours over its own
circle, counting a neighbour only to the extent that neighbour's own blur reaches back: a sharp object in front of a
blurred background does not bleed into it, while a blurred foreground spreads over what is behind it. The sixty-four taps
shift along their spiral by a different fraction at each pixel, which leaves a fine grain that `taa` averages away.

**The bokeh** is the shape of the lens: `blades` of 3 or more makes the aperture a polygon of that many sides (0 is round), turned
by `bladeRotation`, and a bright point out of focus becomes that shape. `highlight` makes bright taps count for more, so a lamp
out of focus is a bright shape and not a dim smear.

### The G-buffer

Some effects have to know what a surface IS, not only how it looks. An effect whose shader says `#pragma gbuffer` makes the
frame draw a second colour attachment beside the scene's, in the same pass and from the same shaders: **octahedral normal in
`r` and `g`, roughness in `b`, metalness in `a`** (`a` is 0 where nothing wrote, and `0.75 + 0.25 × metalness`, so an edge that multisampling averaged with an unwritten pixel (at most 0.5) is told from a surface
where something did), 8 bits a channel. The effect finds it at `u_gbuffer` (GLSL) or `t6` (WGSL, read with `textureLoad`).

It costs one extra RGBA8 target and one extra write per pixel, and only while an enabled effect asks: a frame with no such
effect has no attachment and compiles no variant. What writes it today is the built-in **Model** shader — what an imported
glTF material becomes, with roughness, metalness and normal map as the material says — and the engine's plain lit mesh, which records
what it is: a rough dielectric. A sprite, a blended surface, an unlit mesh and a custom shader leave it at 0, and an effect must
treat that as "unknown" and leave the pixel alone, as `ssr` does.
A shader of your own can write it: it is compiled with `ES_GBUFFER` defined when the pass has the attachment, and then declares
a second output (`layout(location = 1) out vec4`, or a struct return in WGSL) and writes the same encoding.

On WebGL2 and WebGPU alike the attachment is multisampled with the scene and resolved with it. A blended draw never writes
it — a normal averaged with the one behind it is neither's — and the scene's own background clears it to 0.

### Voxel GI

`voxelGI` is diffuse global illumination from a **world-space voxel cache**. A grid of voxels (128 × 64 × 128 around the camera, `voxelSize`
world units each) remembers what the camera has seen: every frame the lit picture is written into the voxels its visible surfaces
stand in — their average radiance and the way they face — and what was there before fades by `decay`. Light is then gathered the way a
path tracer would, but through the grid: from each visible surface a few cosine-weighted rays (`rays`) are marched cell by cell, and
where one meets a voxel that faces it, that voxel's radiance is the light it brings. The gather runs at a fraction of the picture's size
(`divisor`), is blended over frames (`blend`) and is upsampled with the depth, so it does not bleed across an edge.

Because the cache is the world's and not the screen's, a bright wall that has left the picture still tints the floor beside it, a surface lit
only by another is lit, and a moving light lights its surroundings — the three things the screen-space bounce (`ssgi`) cannot do.

The defaults are tuned for a world measured in pixels. In a 3D scene where a world unit is a metre, scale the lengths to match: for a character
shot a `voxelSize` of 0.03 (a cache about four metres across) with a `range` of 48 voxels, and for `ssgi` a `radius` of 0.4 and a `thickness` of
0.15. Both accept values down to a centimetre.

It is **not a trace of the scene's geometry**, and it says so:

- the cache holds the surfaces the camera has *seen*, at the voxel's size — a surface it has not seen yet sends no light, and a thin one is as thick as a cell;
- one bounce, diffuse only (no glossy transport), and the picture it is injected from carries last frame's light, so a bright surface feeds itself a little;
- on WebGPU it runs as compute passes. On **WebGL2**, which has no compute stage, the same cache is built by three fragment passes into one half-float atlas (2048 × 1024, 32 MB): each atlas texel asks the picture what stands in its voxel, so the result matches the WebGPU one but the gather is always at half size (`divisor` is ignored), surfaces seen at a grazing angle are thinner in the cache, and the cost is higher. The `low` quality tier leaves it out on WebGL2;
- it costs a grid of memory (about 32 MB), a few passes a frame and one cache per camera.

It is not Unreal's Lumen: there is no distance-field or hardware ray tracing and no surface cache of the scene's meshes, which is what lets Lumen light what
has never been on screen. Voxel GI is the version of the idea that needs only the picture.

### Compute effects (WebGPU)

An effect can be a **compute shader** instead of a draw: WGSL run over the whole picture, which is how an effect that reads
many neighbours or keeps a result of its own is written without a render pass per step. `sharpen` is one: each pixel is
pushed away from the mean of its neighbours and then held inside the range they span, so an edge gets steeper without a halo.

A compute effect needs a device with a compute stage, which is **WebGPU**. On WebGL2 an effect that has a `fallback` (a fragment pass of the same
arithmetic, as `sharpen` does: the two draw the identical picture) runs that instead; one that has none is left out of the chain and the picture goes
through as it was, so a compute effect without a fallback is for refining a picture that is already whole: write one that way. Ask `postprocess_supportsCompute` (or check the backend) if your game needs to know.

Register your own like any other effect, with `compute` in place of `factory`:

```ts
import { registerEffect } from 'esengine';

registerEffect({
  type: 'myInvert',
  label: 'Invert',
  compute: { source: INVERT_WGSL, workgroupSize: [8, 8] },
  // The params block: these, as f32s, in this order.
  uniforms: [{ name: 'u_amount', label: 'Amount', min: 0, max: 1, step: 0.01, defaultValue: 1 }],
});
```

The shader gets these at group 0, and declares the ones it uses, with an entry point named `main`:

| Binding | What | Declared as |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | the image so far | `texture_2d<f32>` |
| 1 | the image it writes, the same size | `texture_storage_2d<rgba16float, write>` |
| 2 | the effect's `uniforms`, as f32s in listed order | `var<uniform>` of a struct that starts with them |
| 3 | the size, `vec4f(w, h, 1/w, 1/h)` | `var<uniform>` |
| 4 | the scene's depth | `texture_depth_2d` |
| 5 | a linear clamp sampler | `sampler` |

It is dispatched over the whole picture in groups of `workgroupSize`, so it checks that its pixel is inside the image.

### Fog and atmosphere

`heightFog` is fog that thins with height: a valley fills, a hilltop clears. Its density falls
off exponentially, so how much of it a ray crosses has a closed form — no marching — and the same
formula makes the **horizon hazy and the zenith clear**, because a ray skimming the ground stays in
the thick part and one aimed up leaves it at once. Sky pixels have no surface to measure to, so they
take the formula with a very long ray; `skyFog` says how much of that haze the sky keeps. Looking toward
the sun the haze takes `sunColor`: give it the sun's direction as `sunAzimuth` (degrees from +Z toward
+X) and `sunElevation` (degrees above the horizon). With `followSun` on (the default) the effect
ignores them and takes the sun from the scene: the first directional light that shines, its direction
and its colour, so a sun that turns takes its haze with it; the two angles are what a scene with no
directional light falls back to.

`volumetric` makes the haze carry the sun's shadows. The air between the camera and each surface is walked in
24 steps against the sun's shadow map, and every step that stands in light scatters some of it toward the
eye — so a building's shadow is dark fog and the gap between two of them a beam. It needs a directional light
that casts shadows (the effect reads the same atlas the meshes receive from), and `volumeRange` says how far
from the camera the air is walked; beyond it the fog still dims but takes no shafts.

`cloudShade` makes those beams follow the sky's clouds, so a gap in them is a shaft that stands in the air whichever way the camera looks, not
only toward the sun. The fog marches the sun's ray up through the cloud layer of a `skyAtmosphere` with `cloudShadow` above 0 (the clouds are then
fixed to the world and drift with its wind), against the same density the sky draws; what the clouds cover is dark air. Its beams are as long as
`volumeRange`, in world units, and a beam through clouds kilometres up needs air that far up: a fog with a low `heightFalloff` and a `volumeRange` of
a few kilometres. The sky's cover, height, thickness and wind are copied onto the fog each frame; they are not settings of the fog's own.

The clouds may be given their shape by a picture. A `skyAtmosphere` with a **Cloud Mask** (`u_cloudMask`, a tileable coverage texture: linear data, one channel, repeating, 0 clear and 1 dense) and its `cloudMaskTile` (the kilometres of the cloud layer one repeat covers) draws clouds where the mask says, the noise only eroding the edges; a second, softer and higher mask (`u_cloudMask2` with `cloudMask2Tile`) is folded in at a third. The same clouds shape the shadow they cast on the scene and the beams the fog lights, which read the masks off the sky. A mask is data: no sRGB, no compression. It says nothing of height, so give the layer a modest `cloudThickness` (under a kilometre): seen low, a thick layer of it smears into streaks along the line of sight.

`lightShafts` finds the sun's light through whatever stands in front of it, in screen space: each pixel
marches toward the sun's place on screen and counts how much of the way is open sky. It needs the
sun on screen or just off it, and only what the depth buffer holds can cast a shaft. Together the two
give the haze-and-rays look of a low sun; `lightShafts` is still screen-space; the shadowed volume is `heightFog`'s `volumetric`.

Fog colours are authored as **sRGB** and converted for a linear project, the way a clear colour
and a light colour are, so the colour you pick is the colour a fully fogged surface shows.

### Screen-space reflections

`ssr` reflects what is on screen in the surfaces that face up: a wet street, a polished
floor, a lake's edge. The reflected ray is marched through the world
and projected back at every step, and a step that lands just behind what the depth buffer shows
is a hit. What it finds is blended over the pixel by a Schlick Fresnel term: faint face-on,
strong at a grazing angle, so the floor shines toward the horizon. A farther hit is blurred more,
which stands in for roughness.

What a surface is comes from [the G-buffer](#the-g-buffer): where the scene wrote one, the reflection uses the
surface's own normal (a normal map's detail, which depth cannot see), its roughness and its metalness. A rough surface
reflects dimly and blurred, and one rougher than `maxRoughness` is skipped before the ray is marched, which is most of the
cost; a metal reflects nearly all it receives where a dielectric reflects 4% face-on. `wetness` makes ground
smoother (a film of water fills the pores), so it reflects as it gets wet, and puddles are mirrors. Where nothing wrote
a record — a sprite, a blended or unlit surface, a custom shader — nothing is guessed: the pixel is left as it was. `floorBias`
(0 by default) can narrow what reflects further: at 1 only up-facing surfaces do.
Where the ray leaves the picture or goes on to the sky, the surface still reflects the **environment** the frame's meshes
reflect, at the sharpness its roughness gives; `skyReflection` says how much (0 turns it off). So a puddle shows the sky and
what lies beyond the edge of the picture, not only the props on screen. `maxDistance` is a
world length; the step count is fixed, so a longer reach is a coarser march, and the per-pixel
start offset that hides the banding is what `taa` averages away — use them together.

### Temporal anti-aliasing

**Put `taa` after every effect whose noise it should average** — ambient occlusion, contact shadows, global illumination,
reflections, fog. A temporal pass resolves what runs before it; a speckle added after it reaches the screen as it is. In the
Bistro example moving `taa` from second place to after the fog lowered the grain on a flat wall by a quarter. The editor marks a
`taa` that has such an effect after it with a warning tag.

`taa` smooths what MSAA cannot reach — shader aliasing and thin geometry — by
accumulating frames. While the effect is in the chain the engine shifts the picture by
a different sub-pixel amount each frame (eight positions, repeating), keeps the effect's
last output, and blends the two. Where a pixel was last frame comes from the depth
buffer: the pixel is un-projected to the world and projected with the previous
frame's camera, so a moving camera keeps its history. `blend` is the share of each
frame that is new: lower is smoother and slower to follow a change, 1 is no accumulation.

Put it **first** in the chain, so everything after it sees the settled image, and keep
`msaaSamples` on — the two add. Each camera keeps its own history.

The camera's motion says where history was for everything that stood still. An object
that moves by itself is a different case, so while the effect is in the chain the frame
draws the **moving meshes a second time** into a target of its own, writing how far each
pixel travelled since last frame, and the effect follows that instead where it is the
surface the scene shows. This is why a thin, fast mover keeps its contrast instead of
fading into the floor behind it. It costs one extra draw of only what moved (nothing,
for a frame where nothing did) and an RGBA32F target the size of the scene, so it needs
float render targets, and is skipped on a device without them. `objectMotion` at 0
turns it off, which leaves the camera's answer alone. A skinned mesh is followed by its
bones: the frame remembers the palette each one was drawn with last time. What it does not
follow is a blended surface and motion a material's vertex hook adds — those fall back to
the camera's answer, clipped into the colours around the pixel. TAA needs scene depth like SSAO does, so on WebGPU
it cannot run with multisampling on a scene that has no depth resolve.

The uniform names above are the friendly labels; **in code they are `u_`-prefixed**
(`u_intensity`, `u_exposure`, …). Most effects are identity at their defaults
(`colorGrade`, `blur`, `chromaticAberration`, `fxaa`, `lensDistortion`, `lutGrade`),
so adding them changes nothing until you dial them in. A few are visible the moment
you add them — `grayscale` (full desaturation), `vignette`, `bloom`, `outline`, and
`tonemap` (the ACES curve always applies) — so enable those only when you want them.

## HDR (linear color space)

When the project renders in the **linear color space** (see
[Lighting](/docs/graphics/lighting/)), the effect chain runs in **half-float HDR**
wherever float render targets are available — always on WebGPU, and on WebGL2 with
the near-universal `EXT_color_buffer_float`. Light accumulation past `1.0` (a
`Light` with `intensity` above 1) then *survives* into the chain instead of
clamping at the 8-bit store:

- **`bloom` with `threshold` above 1** blooms only over-range energy — bright
  lights glow while ordinary LDR art stays crisp. This is the classic "emissive
  glow" setup: crank a light's `intensity`, set `threshold` ≈ 1.2–1.5.
- **`tonemap`** receives true HDR input, so the ACES curve rolls off highlights
  instead of shaping already-clamped values.
- Without a tonemap pass, over-range values simply clip at the final
  linear→sRGB encode — fine for subtle glow, harsh for large over-exposure.

In gamma mode (or without float-target support) the chain is LDR and behaves as
before; a `threshold` above ~0.75 then never fires.

## Output transform

A scene lit in linear space can hold light brighter than a display can show. The
**output transform** is the one curve the finished frame goes through on its way to
the screen — **once, after every effect** — and it is a project setting, not an
effect: **Settings → Project → Rendering → Output transform**.

| Transform | What it does | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **None** | Encodes only. Values above 1 clip. | Flat 2D art, UI, anything that must round-trip exactly. |
| **ACES** | A filmic shoulder: highlights roll off, shadows deepen. It desaturates as it goes, so a very bright red drifts toward cream. | Lit 3D scenes that want a filmic look. |
| **Neutral** | Khronos PBR Neutral: the same roll-off, but hue and saturation survive. A sunlit red stays red. | Product shots, stylised art, anything whose colours are the point. |

It applies to the editor viewport, to Play and to every export, and changing it
needs no reload. In code: `outputTransform: 'none' | 'aces' | 'neutral'`.

A look authored against the finished picture (a warm LUT from a colourist, a film emulation) belongs after the curve, and no effect in the
chain can be: they all run on scene values, before it. The **Display LUT** effect (`displayLut`) is the last pass's own stage: the picture is
curved, encoded for the display, and then read off a 1024x32 table by the colour it will show: 32 slices along x, blue the slice, red along a slice and green up the picture (an image is read from its bottom).
It adds a texture read to the pass that was running anyway, not a pass, whatever the output transform. A table's texture is data: no compression, no mips and no sRGB. An image of that shape (N slices of N by N, so 1024 by 32 for 32) is adopted with those settings on its own, in the editor and from the command line. In code, `stack.setDisplayLut(textureHandle, amount)`.

<Aside>
The `tonemap` *effect* in the list above is a separate, optional ACES pass inside the
chain. With an output transform set you usually want neither it nor a second curve:
pick the output transform and leave the effect off.
</Aside>

## Using a `PostProcessVolume`

In the editor a volume's effects are listed in the order they run, each with its switch and its parameters (colour parameters
included), a fold to hide them, and a tag for what it is for (Lens, Colour, Light, Atmosphere, Anti-aliasing, Stylize); an
effect that needs a compute shader says **WebGPU only**. The add menu groups the effects the same way.

Add a `PostProcessVolume`, enable effects, and set their parameters. Volumes
**blend where they overlap**, so you can drive effects by area (a "underwater"
volume) or fade them in over a transition.

| Property | Type | Default | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| `effects` | list | `[]` | `{ type, enabled, uniforms }` per effect. |
| `isGlobal` | boolean | `true` | Apply everywhere vs. only inside the volume shape. |
| `shape` | `'box' \| 'sphere'` | `'box'` | Bounds shape for a local volume. |
| `size` | Vec2 | `{5, 5}` | Volume extents (local volumes). |
| `priority` | number | `0` | Higher-priority volumes win where they overlap. |
| `weight` | number | `1` | Blend weight, 0..1 (fade the whole volume). |
| `blendDistance` | number | `0` | Falloff distance at the volume edge. |

## Programmatic control

For a fixed camera pipeline in code, use the `PostProcess` resource. A stack starts
empty — add one **pass** per effect. Get the effect's shader from the registry with
`getEffectDef(type).factory()`, then set its `u_`-prefixed uniforms and bind the
stack to a camera:

```typescript
import { defineSystem, Query, Res, Camera, PostProcess, getEffectDef } from 'esengine';

const setup = defineSystem([Query(Camera), Res(PostProcess)], (q, pp) => {
  const grade = getEffectDef('colorGrade')!;
  for (const [camera] of q) {
    const stack = pp.createStack();
    stack.addPass('colorGrade', grade.factory())
         .setUniform('colorGrade', 'u_saturation', 1.3)
         .setUniform('colorGrade', 'u_contrast', 1.1);
    pp.bind(camera, stack);   // pp.unbind(camera) to remove
  }
});
```

A **multi-pass** effect like `bloom` exposes `def.multiPass`; add each sub-pass
(`for (const p of grade.multiPass!) stack.addPass(p.name, p.factory())`). For
anything beyond a fixed pipeline, a `PostProcessVolume` is simpler — it resolves
multi-pass and texture params for you.

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| `PostProcess.createStack()` | Create an empty effect stack. |
| `stack.addPass(name, shader)` | Add an effect pass; `shader` from `getEffectDef(type).factory()` (chainable). |
| `stack.setUniform(pass, uniform, value)` | Set a `u_`-prefixed parameter (chainable). |
| `stack.setTexture(pass, uniform, handle)` | Bind a texture parameter, e.g. a LUT (chainable). |
| `stack.setEnabled(name, on)` | Toggle an already-added pass (chainable). |
| `PostProcess.bind(camera, stack)` / `unbind(camera)` | Attach / remove a camera's stack. |
| `getEffectDef(type)` / `getAllEffectDefs()` / `getEffectTypes()` | Look up effect definitions (shader factory + uniforms). |

### Texture parameters (LUTs & masks)

Some effects take a texture, not just scalars. `lutGrade` samples a **color
lookup table** — a 2D-packed `1024×32` PNG (32 slices of a 32³ cube) — so you can
author a whole grade in an image editor and drop it in. Add the pass, then bind the
texture with `setTexture` (or, on a `PostProcessVolume`, list it under the effect's
`textures: { u_lut: <ref> }`):

```typescript
stack.addPass('lutGrade', getEffectDef('lutGrade')!.factory())
     .setTexture('lutGrade', 'u_lut', lutHandle)
     .setUniform('lutGrade', 'u_intensity', 1);
```

## In a package

An effect's definition (its name and parameters) is in every package; its shaders are most of the bytes, so they come in three groups that a
package carries only when its content uses one:

| Group | Subpath | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| Basic | `esengine/fx-basic` | blur, bloom, vignette, grayscale, colorGrade, lutGrade, agx, chromaticAberration, tonemap, fxaa, sharpen, lensDistortion, pixelate, outline, depthOutline, edgeOutline, filmGrain, motionBlur, lensFlare |
| Atmosphere | `esengine/fx-atmosphere` | mist2d, rain2d, skyAtmosphere, heightFog, distanceFog, rain |
| Lighting | `esengine/fx-lighting` | ssao, gtao, ssr, ssgi, voxelGI, contactShadows, lightShafts, autoExposure, depthOfField, subsurface, taa |

The export reads the effects a `PostProcessVolume` names (and a `Weather` or `TimeOfDay` component, which drive the atmosphere group) and imports
the groups they need. An effect you add **from a script** — `PostProcess.add('bloom')` — leaves no trace in the content, so import its group
(`import 'esengine/fx-basic'`) or set the module to **Include** in Project Settings; otherwise the effect says which group it needs when it is
first used. The editor, and any entry that is not lean, carries all three.

## Best practices

- **Order matters** — passes run in the order you add them (or, on a volume, the
  order of its `effects[]`) and share the buffers, so a sensible chain is
  bloom → tonemap → color grade → vignette.
- **Use volumes for locality** (an area's mood) and the `PostProcess` API for a
  fixed camera pipeline.
- **Fade with `weight`** on a volume for smooth transitions instead of toggling.
- **Keep the stack short** on low-end/mobile — each pass is a full-screen draw.

<Aside type="note">
  Effects are pure-GLSL and data-driven from a registry, so the editor and runtime
  share one list — there's no hard-coded effect set to drift.
</Aside>
