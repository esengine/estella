A rock a hundred units away and a rock two thousand units away are drawn with the
same number of triangles, and at two thousand units almost all of them land inside
a single pixel. **`LODGroup`** lets an object hand over to a cheaper mesh once it
is small enough that nobody can tell — the single biggest saving available to a
scene with many props in it.

In Estella's own third-person sample, a field of 35 rocks drops from
1.9 million triangles to 42 thousand and from 3.5 ms of GPU time to 0.27 ms at ten
thousand props ([the numbers](#what-it-buys)).

## The LODGroup component

`LODGroup` sits on the **same entity as a `MeshRenderer`**. The renderer says what
the object *is* — that mesh is level 0, the full-detail one. The group says what
may stand in for it as it shrinks:

```typescript
world.insert(rock, MeshRenderer, { mesh: 'assets/models/rock-lod0.esmesh', lit: true });
world.insert(rock, LODGroup, {
    lod1: 'assets/models/rock-lod1.esmesh',
    lod2: 'assets/models/rock-lod2.esmesh',
    lod1Size: 0.25,   // below a quarter of the screen height, lod1 takes over
    lod2Size: 0.11,   // below an eighth, lod2
    cullSize: 0.02,   // below a fiftieth, nothing is drawn at all
    hysteresis: 0.1,
});
```

Remove the component and the full-detail mesh comes back — nothing else about the
renderer changes.

## The sizes are fractions of the screen, not distances

`lod1Size` and its siblings are the fraction of the **viewport's height** the
object's bounds cover. `1.0` is an object exactly one screen tall; `0.02` is a
fiftieth of one.

That is deliberately not a distance, because a distance answers the same at every
scale and through every lens:

- a tower and a pebble a hundred metres away are not the same size on screen;
- a 30° field of view shows an object twice as big as a 60° one, from the same spot;
- an orthographic camera's answer does not change with distance at all.

A screen fraction covers all three, and it is free of the resolution too — the
same number is right on a phone and on a desktop.

<Aside type="tip">
To pick thresholds, put the camera where a level's action happens and look at the
object. The size you want is roughly "the fraction of the window's height it
covers"; anything under about 5% is a candidate for the coarsest mesh you have.
</Aside>

## Hysteresis — why the levels do not flicker

A boundary crossed at exactly one number flips back and forth for a camera that is
merely breathing, and a mesh swapping every frame is far more visible than the
swap itself. `hysteresis` makes each boundary asymmetric: an object hands *down* to
the coarser mesh at the threshold, and only comes back up once it has grown past
it by that fraction.

At the default `0.1`, a `lod1Size` of `0.20` means:

- shrinking past **0.20** → level 1 takes over;
- growing back past **0.22** → level 0 returns.

Set it to `0` and you get a bare threshold, which flickers. It applies to
`cullSize` too, so an object at the edge of visibility does not blink.

## What the selection is, and is not

**It belongs to the camera, not the entity.** Two cameras in one frame — a main
view and a minimap, or a split screen — see the same object at two different sizes
and may draw it at two different levels. There is no `currentLOD` on the entity to
read or to set.

**It is measured on the whole group's bounds.** The bounds used to choose are the
union of every level's, so they do not change with the level being drawn. Bounds
read off the current mesh would feed back into the choice that picked it and
oscillate.

**Culling runs first.** An object the frustum already rejected is never measured,
so the cost is paid only for what could have been seen.

**Instancing is unaffected.** Choosing a level changes which geometry a draw names
and nothing else, so props sharing a level still fold into one instanced call.
A field at three levels reaches the GPU as roughly three draws, not as one per prop.

## Authoring rules

- **Fill the slots in order** — `lod1`, then `lod2`, then `lod3`. A slot left empty
  ends the ladder, and anything after it is never reached (the engine warns).
- **Defaults.** `lod1Size` 0.5, `lod2Size` 0.25, `lod3Size` 0.1, `cullSize` 0.02, `hysteresis` 0.1 (0 to 1); the component's `enabled` switch, when off, always draws the renderer's own mesh.
- **The sizes must descend.** `lod2Size` below `lod1Size`, and so on.
- **`cullSize: 0`** disables culling entirely, so the group is never invisible.
- **A group may have no stand-in at all.** With only a `cullSize` it draws the renderer's own mesh while it is big enough and nothing once it is smaller: the right group for a tuft of grass, which has no cheaper version and is not worth drawing past a distance.
- **The levels can be generated at import.** Set **Generate LODs** to 1–3 levels in a model's import settings (or pass `--lods <n>` to `import-model`): each mesh is collapsed into copies with about 50%, 20% and 7% of its triangles, written as `<mesh>_lod<n>.esmesh`, with a `LODGroup` on the entity. Skinned and morphing meshes, and meshes under **LOD Minimum Triangles** (256), keep one level.
- **A skinned group must share one skeleton.** Every level of a skinned mesh needs
  the same joint count; a level that disagrees is drawn in its bind pose, and the
  engine says so.

## What it buys

Measured on a receding field of props, 512×512, WebGL2:

| props | triangles | GPU |
|---|---|---|
| 1 000, always full detail | 195 776 | 0.505 ms |
| 1 000, with `LODGroup` | 4 248 | 0.116 ms |
| 10 000, always full detail | 1 912 960 | 3.595 ms |
| 10 000, with `LODGroup` | 42 392 | 0.27 ms |

Selection costs 0.2–0.3 ms of CPU per ten thousand props, which is the trade:
CPU spent choosing, GPU saved drawing. The exchange rate improves with the count,
which is the point — it is the mechanism that decides whether a field of ten
thousand props is shippable at all.

## Impostors: a picture in place of a far mesh

An `Impostor` beside a MeshRenderer stands a card in for the mesh once the object covers less than `screenSize` of the view.
The card shows a picture of the mesh taken from the direction the view is looking from, so from far away it is the object, at
two triangles.

```typescript
world.insert(tree, Impostor, { screenSize: 0.06, frames: 8, resolution: 1024 });
```

- **The pictures** are taken while the game runs, once per kind: `frames` x `frames` orthographic cameras of the engine's own look
  at a copy of the mesh from the directions of the upper half of a sphere, each into its own cell of one atlas `resolution`
  pixels on a side. They are ordinary cameras, so the pictures have the mesh's material, textures and the scene's light, on
  every backend. Entities with the same mesh, material, texture and colour share one atlas; kinds are photographed one after
  another, a frame or two each.
- **The card** is drawn by the renderer in each view: the picture taken nearest that view's direction, on a square facing it.
  Every card is drawn through the sprite batch, so cards whose atlases fit in one draw's texture slots are one draw. In the
  `impostor` editor check, five objects of five kinds draw as one draw where their meshes, instanced by shape, drew as two.
- **What it cannot do.** A picture keeps the light it was taken in, in the object's own frame: an object turned in the world is lit
  as if the sun had turned with it, so impostors suit objects lit softly or not turned. The picture changes in steps as the view
  goes round (more `frames` is smaller steps), a card casts no shadow and is not seen from below, and the pictures are taken in
  Play and in a built game, not in the editor's own viewport.

## What this is not

`LODGroup` is about **one object at different sizes**. It is not a streaming
system: every level's mesh is resident, nothing is loaded or unloaded, and an
object that is `cullSize`-small is simply not drawn rather than removed from the
world. Combining distant objects into one merged proxy (HLOD) and loading a world
in pieces are separate concerns, and there is no general-purpose HLOD that bakes
arbitrary scene objects into a proxy. What exists is specific: the far versions
of plants (`Foliage`, `PlacedFoliage` — see [Foliage](/docs/graphics/foliage/)),
the terrain's own distance-based quadtree
([Terrain](/docs/graphics/terrain/)), and [static batching](/docs/performance/static-batching/),
which merges different meshes to save draws but does not make them cheaper far away.
