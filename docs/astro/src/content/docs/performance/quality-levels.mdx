A project can ship one picture and run it on a flagship phone and on a six-year-old one. **Quality levels** are how:
three profiles — **Low, Medium, High** — each saying what the renderer spends at that level. The project picks one
(or lets the device pick), and a game that is too slow for its level steps its resolution down on its own.

## Choosing the level

**Settings → Project → Rendering → Device Quality → Quality level:**

| Mode | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Default | Nothing chosen: **Medium on phones and tablets**, Off everywhere else. A mini game keeps its own defaults. |
| Off | No quality management, on every device. The project renders as authored. |
| Low / Medium / High | That level, on every device. |
| Auto | The level comes from the device: the first [device rule](#device-rules) that matches, otherwise memory and core count. |

**Dynamic resolution** lowers the scene's resolution under GPU pressure, down to the level's *Min render scale*, and
brings it back when there is room; the UI stays at display resolution. A scene drawn below the screen's size is stretched back with a Catmull-Rom resampler clamped to the texels around it, which keeps an edge steeper than a bilinear copy does without drawing a halo; a whole-number stretch is an exact copy instead. **Target frame rate** is the frame time it works
to.

## What a level holds

**Quality Levels → Editing level** picks which level the rows below show and change. They are grouped by what they spend:

| Group | Settings |
|---|---|
| Rendering | Render scale, Min render scale, Anti-aliasing (MSAA or Temporal), MSAA, Depth pre-pass, Detail distance, Draw call budget, Texture streaming and its budget, 2D rain shelter mask |
| Shadows | Atlas size, Cell size, Cascades, Distance, 2D soft-shadow samples |
| Reflections and particles | Planar reflection scale, max size and updates per second, Particle limit |
| Water | Water waves (1 to 4), Water ripple cells, and the Displacement, Mirror and Caustics switches. Shown once the level is not Off and the water module is in the build |
| Post-processing | A master switch, and a tick for each effect that runs at this level |

Each level starts from the engine's own values for it, and **only what you change is saved** in `project.esproject`
under `features.rendering.quality.profiles.<level>`. A default the engine improves later reaches a project that never
touched it, and a row set back to its level's default is not a difference.

**Anti-aliasing** is MSAA or Temporal. Temporal draws at one sample and lets the TAA pass smooth from the frames before:
far cheaper at 1080p and somewhat softer, it needs a post-process volume on the camera, and the MSAA row is hidden while it
is chosen. **Depth pre-pass** draws the opaque scene twice, depth alone first, so each pixel is shaded once for the surface in
front. It pays where surfaces overlap (a wood, a crowd of foliage) on a GPU that shades what it rasterises; on one that defers
shading to the end of a tile it is geometry paid for twice. It is off at Low and on at Medium and High. The default anti-aliasing is MSAA at Low (one sample) and High (4), and Temporal at Medium. The pre-pass and the colour pass declare the vertex position invariant on both WebGL2 and WebGPU, so a surface's colour always passes the equal-depth test against its own depth, including on Metal; a Mac that showed dark triangles on terrain with the pre-pass on needs nothing but an up-to-date build.

<Aside type="note">
A row's "default" is the default of the level it is showing, so the same number can be a change at Low and the default
at Medium.
</Aside>

**Detail distance** is the share of its distances a placed plant keeps at the level: at 0.5, a tree that changes to its
cheaper mesh at 60 m and is not drawn as itself past 150 m does so at 30 m and 75 m, and its far cards take over sooner.
Low keeps 0.5, Medium 0.8 and High all of it, so the same world draws a fraction of the triangles on a phone.

**Draw call budget** is the most draws a frame at the level should ask of the GPU: 150 at Low, 400 at Medium and 1,500 at
High. Every pass counts, not only the scene's own list: each shadow cascade draws its casters again, the depth pre-pass draws
the opaque scene again, and post-processing adds a draw per pass. Nothing is held back to meet it. The Profiler's **Render**
module shows the frame's GPU draws against the budget and splits them by pass, and the stats overlay turns the count red past
it. A draw costs the CPU and the driver more than the GPU, and on a phone or in a mini game it is what a 3D scene runs out of
first. To bring it down, see [Static batching](/docs/performance/static-batching/) for many different meshes and fewer shadow
cascades for the shadow pass.

**Texture streaming** holds each texture that only meshes draw at the size its views need. A texture a MeshRenderer draws, as
its texture, its normal map or through its material, is first loaded no larger than 256 texels on a side, so a scene comes up in a
fraction of the memory; then, for every mesh a view draws, the renderer works out how many texels across its textures need for a
texel to be about a pixel there, from the mesh's own UV density, its size and its distance, and four times a second the textures
that are the wrong size are decoded again from their files at the next power of two above that and put behind the same handle. A
texture grows as soon as it is needed bigger and shrinks only after it has been needed smaller for three seconds; one no view
draws for five seconds goes down to 64 texels. It is on at Low and Medium and off at High; **Texture streaming budget** caps what
the streamed textures take together (128 MB at Low, 256 MB at Medium, 0 is no cap), and past it they are held a level softer.

Measured in the editor's Play at Low, in the `texture-streaming` editor check (the engine's own estimate of GPU memory):

| Example | Streaming off | On: first frame | On: ten seconds in | The two pictures, settled |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `dusk-fort` | 285.6 MB | 33.6 MB | 31.0 MB | mean difference 0.28 of 255 |
| `bistro` | 1293.5 MB | 321.1 MB | 449.6 MB (228 textures, 149 moves) | mean difference 1.1 of 255; 0.04% of pixels differ visibly |

**The download.** A web build ships a small copy beside each texture a mesh draws as its surface and that is more than 512 texels
on a side: no larger than 256, in the same format (a KTX2 encoded again in its own mode, a PNG or a JPEG written as what it was),
halved from the shipped file with the box filter the cook's own mip chains use. Streaming starts the texture from the copy, and
fetches the file itself only once a view needs more than the copy holds; a texture nothing comes close to is never downloaded
whole. Exported for the web at Low and run in a headless browser (software rendering, the files served as immutable):

| Example | Texture download, without the copies | With the copies | Files fetched whole |
|---|---|---|---|
| `dusk-fort`, 20 s | 44.8 MB | 14.9 MB (the copies are 0.4 MB of it) | 3 of 12 |
| `bistro`, 20 s | 35.3 MB | 16.1 MB (3.4 MB of copies) | 4 of 155 |
| `bistro`, 120 s | — | 18.8 MB | 15 of 155 |

A headless browser draws in software, a frame or two a second, so it grows textures far slower than a GPU does: `bistro` was still
fetching a file every five seconds or so at two minutes, and these are what was downloaded by then, not what it settles at. On a GPU
in the editor, ten seconds in, 40 of `bistro`'s textures had grown past their copies and their files come to 8.9 MB: with the 3.4 MB
of copies, about 12.4 MB of textures to download where a build without them takes 35.3 MB. The
copies cost the package their own size (430 KB beside `dusk-fort`'s 42.6 MB of textures). A mini game downloads its package whole,
so no copies are made for one; nor for a texture something other than a mesh draws.

What it does not do: until a view has asked for more, a texture is drawn at the size it started at: the first second or two of a scene can
be softer than the rest, and a move costs a decode and an upload on the frame it lands, which shows as a frame or two of tens of
milliseconds while a scene settles. A texture a sprite, a UI element, a tilemap, a particle or a trail also draws is put back at its
size and left there. A KTX2 file is cut at a level without decoding; where the host transcodes KTX2 itself (the native builds) it
cannot be, and the texture is left at its size.

## Leaving effects out

Every post-process effect the engine has is in the list, ticked where the level runs it. An unticked effect is skipped
at that level wherever a camera's volume asks for it — and all of it: an effect with several passes, such as Ambient
Occlusion or Auto Exposure, is removed whole. The manifest holds the effects left out by type:

```json
"quality": { "mode": "auto", "profiles": { "low": { "skipEffects": ["ssr", "taa", "depthOfField"] } } }
```

By default Low leaves out reflections, temporal anti-aliasing, depth of field, light shafts, motion blur, lens flare, GTAO, subsurface scattering and voxel GI; Medium leaves out
reflections and depth of field; High leaves out nothing. These defaults follow the work each effect does per pixel; they
are **not** measured on weak devices. The editor's `fx-cost` check measures each effect's cost on the machine it runs on,
which is a way to find out what yours costs before choosing what a level skips.

## Device rules

Shown when the mode is Auto. Each row names a level and the conditions a device must meet: **Model contains**,
**GPU contains**, **Max RAM** and **Max cores**, all of which must hold. The first row that matches decides the level. A
row with no condition would match every device, so it must have one.

## Shader compilation and render on demand

Two groups on the same page hold choices about how a game is *delivered* rather than which level it runs at. They apply
whatever the quality level, **Off** included, and are saved under `features.rendering.quality`.

![Project Settings, Rendering page: the Shader Compilation, Render on Demand, Power Saving and Antialiasing groups](@/assets/guides/en/settings-shader-compilation.png)

**Shader Compilation**

| Setting | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Compile shaders in the background** (`asyncShaderCompile`) | A draw whose shader the browser is still compiling is left out, so the game opens at once and fills in as shaders are ready. Off, the first draw to need a shader waits for it: every frame is complete, but a first visit can stall for seconds. Needs `KHR_parallel_shader_compile`; without it shaders compile as before. |
| **Warm up behind the loading screen** (`prewarmShaders`) | Draws the first scene out of sight, behind the loading screen, until no shader is compiling, then opens on a complete picture. Shown once background compilation is on. |
| **Warm-up timeout** | The longest the loading screen waits (in seconds, default 30) before opening anyway. Shown once warm-up is on. |

**Render on Demand**

| Setting | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Draw only changing frames** (`renderOnDemand`) | A still screen costs the game's logic and nothing else. A frame is drawn when something asked for it, the player did something, the surface changed size, entities were created or destroyed, or shaders are still compiling. |
| **Frames kept after a change** (`renderOnDemandTail`) | Frames still drawn after the last reason to draw, so a fade or a settling motion finishes. Default 30. |

The engine cannot see what a script changes by itself (a colour, an animation). Say so with `RenderDemand.request()` for
one frame, or `RenderDemand.hold()` (it returns the function that releases it) for something that moves on its own, or
that change is not drawn until something else wakes the screen. That is why the switch is opt-in.

## Saving power

A long session on a phone is heat and battery, and the frame is where both go. Turn on **Save power when the battery is low or the device is hot** (`QualityConfig.power`) and the engine
watches the device every few seconds: while it is saving it lowers the frame cap and puts a ceiling on the quality level, and when the device recovers it puts both back. Off (the default), the device
is not watched and nothing changes.

| Setting | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Battery below** (`lowBattery`) | Saves while the battery is under this share and not charging. Default 20%; 0 never saves for the battery. |
| **Thermal pressure** (`thermal`) | Saves when the platform reports pressure of this level or worse: Serious (default), Critical, or Never. |
| **Frame cap while saving** (`fps`) | Frames a second while saving. Default 30. A cap the game already set lower is kept. |
| **Level ceiling while saving** (`ceiling`) | No level above this while saving; a level already under it is left. Default Medium. |

At Critical thermal pressure it goes further: the cap is halved (never under 15) and the level is held at Low (`severe: 'never'` turns that off). What a platform does not report never triggers
anything: a browser with no battery API saves nothing for the battery, and most browsers report no heat at all (Chromium reports the battery and the processor's pressure; the WeChat mini-game
platform reports the battery). `Device.power()` gives a game the same numbers (`level`, `charging`, `thermal`, each `null` where the platform does not say), and `Quality`'s report names `power-saver`
among its limitations while it is holding the level down. The cap is put back only if the game has not changed it itself since.

For a still screen (a menu, a card table) the better saving is to draw nothing: see render-on-demand in the same page of the editor's settings.

## In code

The same settings are `QualityConfig` — `QualityController`, `parseQualityConfig`, `resolveQualityProfile` and
`defaultQualityProfile` build and read them — and the running level is the `Quality` resource.
